Internal teams receive work orders the same way suppliers do — assigned by a dispatcher, generated by a PM schedule, or routed by dispatch rules — but with your organization's full context, since you're inside the account.

## Your queue

Your assigned work orders show what you need to plan a day: location, problem, priority, scheduled windows, and everything already known about the job — photos from the site, the asset's history, notes from whoever reported it, and troubleshooting already attempted.

## Working a job

<Steps>
  <Step title="Review before you drive">
    The work order tells you what you're walking into — and the asset history often tells you what part you'll need. A minute of reading beats a second trip.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check in with WrenchMode">
    On arrival, check in. Your time on site — and drive time between jobs — is tracked automatically. See [WrenchMode](/internal-teams/wrenchmode).
  </Step>
  <Step title="Do the work, log the work">
    Notes, photos, and parts used. If you need parts you don't have, raise a [purchase request](/internal-teams/purchase-requests) from the job.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Close it out">
    Completion notes and after-photos. If the job needs a return visit or a specialist supplier, say so — the work order routes onward instead of silently dying.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Tasks within work orders

Larger jobs can carry **tasks** — checklist items within the work order — so multi-step work (or work split across teammates) tracks step by step instead of as one opaque blob.

## When to escalate to a supplier

Some jobs turn out to be beyond in-house scope — refrigerant work needing certification, warranty repairs, specialized equipment. Flag it on the work order with what you found; the dispatcher reassigns to the supplier network with your diagnosis attached, so the vendor arrives ahead of the game.

<Note>
  Your documentation is what makes the in-house team's value visible. Checked-in hours, completed jobs, and first-time fixes are how the organization sees — and grows — its internal capability.
</Note>
